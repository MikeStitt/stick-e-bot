# The robot — build plan and course coverage

An articulated figure, about 300 mm tall, modeled the way the Onshape courses teach it. 

The "about 300 mm" above is a target the stations were fitted to in an early version of the figure.
There is no requirement for the exact height of the figure because driving the design to a specific
height forces unneeded work. The program `make_plans.py` determines the sizes.

This plan exists
to answer one question: **how much of the official curriculum can one model actually carry,
and what does it leave out?**

Coverage is measured against the three courses as crawled from the Learning Center — every
lesson name, every Key Takeaway, and all fourteen slides of *Available Constraints*. The
checklists below are their lesson lists, not a paraphrase.

## Nothing here is settled until the whole robot is printed

**`task.print.whole_robot`: print the entire robot, assemble it, and operate it.** Every number in
this plan and in the briefs it points at has been decided in CAD, checked against arithmetic, and
in a few places measured on a printed part. None of that is the same as a robot that prints, snaps
together and still holds its pose after a class has played with it.

**Where a calculation and a print disagree, the print wins.** The hinge's fingers are the worked
example. Run 5 built the ear at **1.2**, and the arithmetic said that was safe — an 8 % margin on
stress. Printed, those fingers splayed over the axle stubs and stayed splayed. The calculation was
not wrong; it asked whether the finger would break, and the finger did not break, it stopped
coming back. The ear is 3.0 because of that print. See
[`experiments/build-briefs/hinge.md`](experiments/build-briefs/hinge.md).

**What the calculations here leave out**, and what a print would put back: whether a finger bends
along its length or at a root, permanent set after the first assembly, infill ratio, infill
pattern, and which axis each part is printed on. A number for one aspect of a printed assembly is
one aspect, and this robot has many that nothing here is paying attention to.

## The figure

Thirteen joints, of two kinds.

| Joint | Count | Type | Freedom |
| ----- | ----- | ---- | ------- |
| Neck | 1 | ball and socket | 3 rotations |
| Shoulder | 2 | ball and socket | 3 rotations |
| Elbow | 2 | **hinge, detented** | 1 rotation |
| Wrist | 2 | ball and socket | 3 rotations |
| Hip | 2 | ball and socket | 3 rotations |
| Knee | 2 | **hinge, detented** | 1 rotation |
| Ankle | 2 | ball and socket | 3 rotations |

Elbows and knees are fork-and-blade hinges: a forked end on one limb, a flat tongue on the
other. **There is no pin.**

**The hinge started as LEGO's click joint and stopped being one.** The blade carries a Ø4 axle
standing 3.00 off each face of its tongue; each fork ear is bored Ø4.1 straight through on the same
line. Pinch the two leaves together, slide the tongue in, let go — the axle springs into the bores.
The mating faces carry **24 wedges, 15° apart**, on the tongue and on the ear alike: pie slices with
45° sides standing 0.75 proud, so a detent is one member's wedge sitting between two of the other's.
The joint clicks between positions and *holds a pose*. A friction hinge goes slack; a detented one
does not, and being poseable is the whole point of the figure.

**There are no holes in the joint, and that is the reason for the change.** The detent used to be a
cone dropping into a hole bored through the ear. The printer put supports in those holes, they could
not be cleaned out, and the printed joint held 210 N·mm where it was drawn for 454 — one extrusion
width of residue in a valley accounts for the whole loss. A protrusion has nothing to clean out.

Both halves are springs, and that is what lets a hand assemble it: a slit up the middle of the
tongue splits it into two leaves, tapered 4.20 at the root to 1.50 at the tip, and the leaf and the
ear share the movement in series. It takes **75.2 N** to pinch together and holds **641 N·mm**, or
1332 N·mm once friction on the 45° flanks is counted. The specification is
[`experiments/build-briefs/hinge.md`](experiments/build-briefs/hinge.md), the sweep that settled
every number is [`reviews/hinge/`](reviews/hinge/), and this section is the reasoning behind it.

What that trades away, stated plainly: a snapped pin used to come from the LEGO bin, and a
snapped stub means reprinting a limb. The 3.2 mm bar now has exactly one consumer — the gripper —
which is a cleaner story than the same number turning up in two unrelated places.

### Joint parts are added to main parts

A joint portion is not a detail of a part — it is **a part in its own right, added to a main
part.** A ball stud is modeled on its own and added to the torso. A socket is modeled on its
own and added to the head.

**Say "added" even when the Boolean is a subtract.** The socket is a part you add to the head;
that it arrives as a cavity is how it is built, not what it is. Keeping the word steady means
one sentence covers all fourteen, and it matches how the feature tree reads.

| Joint part | Added to | Arrives as |
| ---------- | -------- | ---------- |
| neck ball stud | torso | material |
| shoulder ball stud | torso | material |
| hip ball stud | torso | material |
| wrist ball stud | forearm | material |
| ankle ball stud | shin | material |
| neck socket | head | a cavity |
| shoulder socket | upper arm | a cavity |
| hip socket | thigh | a cavity |
| wrist socket | gripper | a cavity |
| ankle socket | foot | a cavity |
| elbow fork | upper arm | material |
| knee fork | thigh | material |
| elbow blade | forearm | material |
| knee blade | shin | material |

On [sheet 2](experiments/build-briefs/images/plan-parts.png) each of these is marked
with a **+**, so the drawing reads as *main part, plus the joint parts added to it.*

**Handedness is a design decision, and the answer is no.** A part that is its own mirror image
serves both sides; one that is not doubles the part count for nothing. So the foot is symmetric
about its own fore-and-aft centerline, and the gripper's C-clip opens forward rather than
outward.
Neither choice costs the design anything, and each one halves a part. Getting the part count
down by shaping the part is what a designer actually does, and it is invisible unless somebody
says it out loud.

**The feet are not handed, and they are allowed to touch.** The ankle socket sits on the foot's own
left-right centerline as well as its fore-and-aft one, so nothing in the part says which leg it
belongs to. Each foot then sits where its own leg does, at x ±24, and a 48 mm foot brings the two
inner edges together at x 0. **Feet touching when the legs and ankles hang straight down from the
hips is a stance the design accepts.** Every joint below the hip turns, so some pose always brings
the feet together; buying one pose back costs a left foot and a right foot, which is the thing this
design does not do.

**Everything is modeled in place, on the right-hand side.** Each limb sits at its own station
on the layout sketch, so a ball stud on the torso is already in position to be subtracted from
the socket that receives it. The left-hand stations stay empty in the Part Studio; the assembly
fills them.

## Variables

Every number the model dimensions with is declared in one of two places, and which of the two is a
rule rather than a preference: **a dimension read by two Part Studios is declared in the Variable
Studio.** Onshape scopes a variable to the Part Studio that declares it, so a second tab that needs
the same number has to re-declare it, and re-declaring is what lets two copies of one number drift
apart. Everything read by one tab stays in that tab, and the **Proportions** table below records
what each of those came from, so a later run can promote one without re-deriving it.

**The numbers here are the robot as it is built, at `#torsoH` 96.** This section carried the 48 mm
robot until draft9p1: the doubling reached `make_plans.py` and the build briefs and never reached
the document the Constitution calls the design source.

### The four that drive the robot's size

| Variable | mm | Meaning |
| -------- | -- | ------- |
| `#torsoH` | 96 | torso height |
| `#torsoW` | 72 | torso width — three quarters of the height, at the default proportions |
| `#torsoD` | 48 | torso depth — half the height, at the default proportions |
| `#limbCenter` | 48 | one limb segment, joint center to joint center |

`#limbCenter` spaces the joints, not the stock: hip to knee, knee to ankle, shoulder to elbow
and elbow to wrist are all this far apart, and each rod is then cut to whatever length puts
its two joint centers there. No two of the four rods come out the same length, because a
socket sinks its center inside the face and a ball stud stands its center clear of one.

The three torso numbers are independent, not expressions on each other. A student who wants a
stocky robot edits the width; one who wants a lanky one edits the height and `#limbCenter`.
That is the whole variable lesson the class spends time on.

### The ones the rule brings up with them

Two or more Part Studios read each of these, so each has one row in the Variable Studio and none in
the tabs that use it. The **read by** column is the reason the row is here.

| Variable | mm | From | Read by |
| -------- | -- | ---- | ------- |
| `#ball` | 12 | `#torsoH / 8` | `body`, `ball and socket`, `foot`, `gripper` |
| `#limbD` | 24 | `#torsoH / 4` | `body`, `hinge`, `u limb`, `l limb` |
| `#wall` | 1.8 | `#torsoH * 3 / 160` | `ball and socket`, `foot`, `gripper` |
| `#stand` | 10 | `#ball * 5 / 6` | `body`, `l limb` |
| `#collar` | 10 | `#stand`, so both halves of a joint reach the same distance | `ball and socket`, `foot` |
| `#fit` | 0.08 | **not scaled**; the printer decides it | `ball and socket`, and `#grip` in the row below |
| `#ballLoss` | 0.10 | **not scaled**; what a printed ball loses on its radius | `#grip` and `#mouth` |
| `#t_print` | 0.10 | **not scaled**; how far one printed surface lands off where it was drawn | `hinge` |
| `#grip` | 2.2205 | `sqrt((#ball / 2 + #fit) ^ 2 - (0.48 * #ball - #ballLoss) ^ 2)` | `ball and socket`, `foot` |
| `#blade` | 10 | typed; the hinge's tongue, and the width every other hinge number is set from | `hinge`, `l limb` |
| `#wedge_h` | 0.75 | typed; how far a wedge stands proud of its face | `hinge`, and `#gap` two rows below |
| `#wedge_c` | 0.15 | **not scaled**; the air over a wedge tip, which the printer decides | `hinge`, and `#gap` in the row below |
| `#gap` | 0.90 | `#wedge_h + #wedge_c` | `hinge`, and `#seat` in the row below |
| `#seat` | 11.80 | `#blade + 2 * #gap` | `hinge`, `u limb` |
| `#blade_out` | 32 | typed; how far the blade stands out of its own limb | `hinge`, `l limb` |
| `#slot_deep` | 33 | `#blade_out + 1` | `hinge`, `u limb` |
| `#tab_free` | 20 | `#blade_out - #limbD / 2` | `hinge`, `l limb` |
| `#ear_free` | 21 | `#slot_deep - #limbD / 2` | `hinge`, `u limb` |
| `#flat` | 10.4494 | `sqrt((#limbD / 2) ^ 2 - (#seat / 2) ^ 2)` | `hinge`, `u limb`, `l limb` |

**The hinge's rows are here because the hinge stopped being one Part Studio's business.** A limb
reads `#ear_free` or `#tab_free` to know where its rod stops, and both limbs read `#flat` to cut the
same two faces the fork is cut on, so a limb that types those numbers drifts from the joint it
carries the moment either moves. `#blade`, `#wedge_h` and `#wedge_c` come with them because the
rows above are computed from them and a Variable Studio row cannot read a Part Studio's.

**`#gap` stopped being a fit and became a space.** It used to be 0.15, the clearance the printer had
to hold between two faces resting on each other. The two faces no longer touch: each carries a ring
of wedges standing `#wedge_h` proud, and `#gap` is what that ring needs to stand in plus the
`#wedge_c` of air left over a tip at a detent. `#wedge_c` is the row the printer decides now, and it
has to clear twice the printer's error, because two surfaces face each other.

**Which tutorial types a row into the studio is not settled here.** The studio is one element and
rows go into it at any point in the build.
[`build/plan/01-torso.md`](build/plan/01-torso.md) puts the four drivers and `#wall` in the first
tutorial; the other six arrive with the parts that read them.

**Two rows carried a *not scaled* mark that did not hold.** `#wall` did scale, and the rule it
scales by has since changed twice: `#torsoH / 32` gave 3.0, and `#torsoH * 3 / 160` gives 1.8, which
is the wall `src/stickbot/socket_spring.py` says the socket needs. `#fit` does not scale and it
moved anyway: 0.2 here, 0.8 as draft9p0 built it, 0.08 now. Marking a number *not scaled* records
where it came from; it does not stop anyone changing it.

**`#collar` was typed with no reason to be, and is not any more.** It was 5.5 at the 48 mm robot
and 11 at this one, with nothing written down that produced both, so driving `#torsoH` past 96 grew
the ball it wraps and left the collar where it was. It is now `#stand`: a socket reaches back from
the ball's center exactly as far as a stud stands its ball clear of its own face, so both halves of
a joint give up the same length of limb and the joint is symmetric about the center it turns on.

### The ones the ball and socket keeps

Nothing outside the joint builds geometry from these, so they stay in the Part Studio that does.

| Variable | mm | From | Meaning |
| -------- | -- | ---- | ------- |
| `#stalk` | 6 | `#ball / 2` | the neck the ball stands on |
| `#slit` | 1.6 | typed | how wide each relief slit is |
| `#stud_len` | 10 | scaffolding | the stud's top face above the ball's center; on the robot the stalk grows out of a torso face instead |
| `#slit_in` | 3.679 | `sqrt((#ball / 2 + #fit) ^ 2 - (#ball / 3) ^ 2) - #wall / 2` | where a slit's inner end stops. Inside the mouth's 5.66, or the mouth cannot open, and inside the cavity where the slit bottoms out, or the cut ends blind |
| `#slit_d` | 6.2205 | `#grip + #ball / 3` | how deep a relief slit is cut, which is the fingers' free length |
| `#mouth` | 11.32 | `0.96 * #ball - 2 * #ballLoss` | the opening the ball snaps through |
| `#slit_out` | 12 | clear of the rim | where the slit ends — the collar's radius is 7.8, so it overshoots by 4.2 |

`#slit_out` and `#slit_d` were plain numbers through run 6 and got rows in run 8p1, so that the
slit sketch has no bare numbers left in it at all.

**`#stud_len` and `#mouth` are recorded here and are not variables in the tab.** The stud's top
face is `#stand` above the ball's center, which is what `#stand` means, so a second row for the same
height would be a number with two homes. The mouth is where the cavity sphere crosses the socket's
top face, so it follows from `#grip` and cannot be set independently of it.

**The clip radius has a row for the first time.** It is the one number in the robot set by something
outside it — a Ø3.2 LEGO bar — and it sat in neither table while both tables described numbers that
scale.

**The ear row used to read 1.2, and 1.2 was the ear that failed.** Run 5 printed a fork with an
ear that thick and the fingers splayed over the axle stubs and stayed splayed. The ear is
`(#limbD - #seat) / 2` and comes out 6.10, where `#seat` is the slot: 11.80, one width from the
fork's tip to its root. It fell from 6.85 when `#gap` grew and took 0.10 back when `#gap`
shrank, and the ear can afford either: the ear was never the soft member. The slot used to be
cut 11.2 with a raised land around the pin giving the fit back over the 20.8 the teeth needed;
the land came out on 2026-09-02, and the face it stood on came forward to where it was. Nothing
seats on that face now — it stands `#gap` off the tongue's and the two rings of wedges reach
across.

**The blade row recorded the other hinge.** `#torsoH / 16` gives 6.0 at this size, which is the
blade in `stickbot-for-bot-review`; `make_plans.py` draws 10.0 and draft9p0 built 10.0.
[`experiments/build-briefs/hinge.md`](experiments/build-briefs/hinge.md) carries both in one table
and says the row is not settled. The number above is 10.0, because that is what the sheets draw and
what the model is being edited to.

**Two rows became one.** Hip half-spacing and leg centerline were separate rows carrying the same
12, derived two different ways. They are one expression, `#torsoW / 2 - #limbD / 2`, and it is the
one that puts a leg's outer surface on the torso's side at any width —
[`experiments/runs/2026-08-25-draft9p1/a7-hip-shoulder.md`](experiments/runs/2026-08-25-draft9p1/a7-hip-shoulder.md)
has what the 1× robot got by coincidence instead.

**The arm centerline row is gone, because the arm has no centerline.** It read 27 — a shoulder
half-spacing plus a limb radius plus 3 mm of air — and it described an arm hanging square to a
torso. The shoulder is a stud leaving the side face 53° below horizontal and 30° toward the front,
so the arm hangs 33.13° off vertical in a plane 7.82 forward of the torso's center. What that row
was protecting is now the shoulder stud's own length: at 26 along the stud the ball stands 13.55
clear of the side face, the arm's whole arc is free of the torso, and the closest it comes anywhere
in that arc is 1.46.

**What *not scaled* means, now that `#wall` has lost the mark.** A number set by the printer, the
plastic or LEGO does not belong with the four that drive the robot's size, and `#barD` and `#fit`
are the two that qualify. That is a reason not to make one a driving variable; it is not a reason
not to make one a variable at all. Both have rows, because the model that uses them has to record
why they are what they are — a different job from making the robot bigger.

### Why the limbs are Ø12

Decided 2026-08-11. **This reverses a change made earlier the same day** — the limbs went to
Ø16 and came back — and the reversal is the instructive part.

**The robot has since doubled and the limbs are Ø24.** Everything below is the argument as it was
made, at the 48 mm robot. It is an argument about proportion rather than about 12: `#limbD` is
`#torsoH / 4`, and every fraction in it holds at either size.

The hinge build measured **0.200 mm of clevis wall per side** inside a Ø12 limb, and observed
that the clevis is a **12 × 11.6 paddle whose corners stood up to 2.5 mm proud** of the rod.
Both facts were real. The mistake was the response: we grew the limb to fit the joint, rather
than asking why the joint was that size.

The joint was that size because it had been **scaled off the torso instead of off the nozzle**.
A 6 mm blade is fifteen extrusion widths — far more material than a hinge this small needs.
Redesigning from the printer upward gives a **7.0 mm fork inside Ø12** with 2.2 mm of wall, and
then a second change removes the constraint altogether:

**The fork is drawn as slices of the limb, not as a rectangle inside it.** Each layer runs out
to the Ø12 arc instead of being cut off at a common width, so there is no rectangle, no corner,
and nothing to check. The ear gains 13 % of cross-section for free, and the blade's own corners
land exactly on the rod's surface.

| | Ø12, joint redesigned | Ø16, joint unchanged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clevis wall per side | 2.2 mm | 2.2 mm |
| Fraction of 48 | 48/4 | 48/3 |
| Legs against the torso sides | flush | 2 mm proud |
| Ball joint sweep | **78°** | 61° |
| Limb aspect over a 24 segment | 2 : 1 | 1.5 : 1 |

**The ball joint prefers Ø12 too, and that overturns the argument used to justify Ø16.** The
claim was that Ø16 gave the socket collar a bigger step-back — 3.3 mm a side against 1.3. True,
but backwards: the joint stops when the limb's own rim swings up to meet the mating part, and a
wider limb puts that rim further out. **What buys the swing is the collar's height, not the gap
around it.** Boring the socket straight into the limb face gives 56°; standing the collar
5.5 mm proud gives 78°.

**Two stations are relations rather than coordinates, and one of the two survived:**

- Leg centerline = shoulder half-spacing − limb dia/2 = 12. A leg's outer surface lands on the
  torso's side — true at Ø12 by coincidence before, stated now. It is `#hip_half` today, and 24.
- Arm centerline = shoulder half-spacing + limb dia/2 + 3 = 27, keeping 3 mm of air. This one went
  when the shoulder moved onto an angled stud: the arm hangs 33.13° off vertical, so it has no
  centerline, and the clearance is bought by the stud's length instead.

Both were hard-coded when the diameter changed, and had to be recomputed by hand because nothing
recorded what they were derived from. Written down as relations, the next change to the diameter is
arithmetic somebody redoes rather than a discrepancy somebody has to find.

**Settled by run 3, and it is why the part is called a gripper.** The C-clip is Ø10 against a
Ø12 wrist, so the widest thing on the arm is the limb and everything below it tapers. That is
why it does not read as a hand, and no diameter fixes it that the socket survives — Ø14 wants
the gripper length at 17.2. The clip radius has a row of its own now, at 5.

**96 is chosen, not rounded.** 2⁵ × 3 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24 and 32, so every
fraction of the torso lands on a whole millimeter — no `33.75` anywhere in the model, and no
dimension that reads like a rounding error. The robot stands **318**, and the stations are what
`make_plans.py` draws:

| Station | mm from ground |
| ------- | -------------- |
| ankle | 24 |
| knee | 72 |
| gripper, bottom, elbow bent 45° | 86.58 |
| mid thigh | 96 |
| wrist | 110.06 |
| hip | 120 |
| elbow | 157.04 |
| shoulder | 197.24 |
| neck | 236 |
| top of head | **318** |

**Every station up the spine is whole, and none of the arm's is.** The legs are two `#limbCenter`
segments and an ankle, all fractions of `#torsoH`, and the head sits a `#collar` and a `#torsoW`
above the neck. The arm hangs off a stud at 53° and 30°, so its stations are that stud resolved into
two directions, and no angle that is not 0, 30, 45, 60 or 90 gives a whole number of millimeters.

**The arms reach mid-thigh, and that is still a relation rather than a number.** Shoulder to the
bottom of the gripper is `2 × #limbCenter` + `#gripperL`, 120 along a straight arm; the arm hangs
33.13° off vertical, so it drops 100.49 and the gripper's bottom lands at **96.74** against a
mid-thigh of 96. The relation was set on an arm hanging square to the torso and the angled stud
cost it 0.74 mm.

The torso comes out **48 × 72 × 96** — a 2 : 3 : 4 box — and the foot is 96 long, the same as
the torso is tall. Proportions worth being able to say out loud are proportions students
remember.

The bar diameter is marked **not scaled** for a reason. Scale the robot to twice the size and
the gripper still has to grip a 3.2 mm bar, because the bar does not scale. A model that teaches
nothing else about interfaces teaches that.

### Why any, when the courses teach none

None of the three courses mentions variables — the word does not appear once across 72
lessons. Four drive the robot anyway, because the torso box and the limb length are the numbers a
student changes to make the robot their own, and typing each of them in six places is how a robot
ends up with a 36 mm torso and a 33.75 mm hole in it.

The joint's own five, and the six that come up into the studio with the drivers, are there for
the other half of what a variable is for. A student who reads
`#collar - #wall` in a dimension box learns the depth of the cut from the box, which is a lesson
one Part Studio can teach in the time it takes to type it.

Everything in the **Proportions** table is declared in the one Part Studio that reads it, and its
proportion is recorded there rather than carried by the Variable Studio: the class gets the lesson
that one number can drive a shape without spending the first tutorial typing twenty rows. The rule
that keeps it honest is unchanged — **variables carry proportion, the layout sketch carries
position.** Anything that says *where* lives in the sketch as a constraint.

## The layout sketch

Everything hangs off a single fully-constrained sketch on the Front plane, made of
construction geometry: a vertical centerline, a horizontal station line at every joint
height, and a point at every joint center. It carries no shape at all.

**The origin sits at the center of the torso**, placed by a center point rectangle. That makes
the torso symmetric about the origin in both directions with no dimension at all, and it is
the part that gets fixed to the assembly origin later — which is exactly what the Assemblies
course means by "always fix a single part to the assembly origin". The ground line therefore
sits at a negative station, −84 mm, and that is fine.

Every later sketch reaches back with **Use (Project/Convert)** and constrains its geometry to
the projection. Three rules make it hold:

1. **Nothing downstream carries its own position dimension.** If a sketch needs to know where
   the knee is, it projects the knee point.
2. **Left and right are `Symmetric` about the centerline**, never dimensioned separately.
3. **Segments that should match are `Equal`** — thigh to shin, upper arm to forearm.

## Four set-pieces about constraints

The course teaches constraints as a topic. These four make them the lesson: one that builds a
part out of constraints alone, two that diagnose a sketch, and one about choosing where the
sketch lives in the first place.

### 1 — The sloppy quadrilateral

Draw four lines into a closed, deliberately irregular quadrilateral. No care, no aim. Then
square it up with constraints alone:

- **Parallel** — top to bottom, then left to right
- **Perpendicular** — one corner is enough; the parallels carry the other three
- Two **dimensions** — width and height

Four lines and three constraints turn a scribble into a rectangle, and the geometry *moves* as
each one lands. It is the most direct demonstration in the whole course that constraints are
instructions, not annotations.

**It no longer has a part to become.** The result used to be the shin, which is why it was not a
throwaway; at Ø12 the shin is a cylinder. The set-piece is worth keeping on its own merits and it
needs somewhere to land — an open decision, see Stage 5.

> **A naming trap.** Onshape's **Normal** constraint is for a line and a curve, or a curve and
> a plane — not two straight lines. Squaring a quadrilateral is **Perpendicular**. Normal gets
> its honest use where a straight limb meets a curved face.

### 2 — Diagnosing under-constrained: drag it

A blue sketch has freedom left in it. The way to find out *what* freedom is to grab an entity
and pull. Whatever moves is what nothing is holding.

Taught as a habit rather than a fact: before accepting any sketch, drag a corner. If the
sketch is black, nothing moves and the drag costs a second. If something slides, that is the
constraint you have not added yet.

### 3 — Diagnosing over-constrained: interrogate it

When Onshape refuses a constraint, the question is which existing one already says that. Two
moves, both straight from the *Using Constraints* lesson:

- **Click an entity** to see every constraint attached to it.
- **Click a constraint icon** to see which entities it holds.

Tick **Show constraints** in the sketch dialog to see them all at once. Delete a constraint by
selecting its icon and pressing Delete.

Worth engineering deliberately: over-constrain a sketch on purpose — dimension a rectangle's
width, its height, *and* its diagonal — then find and remove the redundant one.

### 4 — Choosing the sketch plane, and diagnosing the wrong one

A sketch lies **in** its plane. Anything brought in with `Use` from somewhere else arrives as
its *shadow* on that plane — projected, not copied. That is not a trap to route around; it is
the rule, and it is learnable in one sentence:

> **Sketch on the face that is parallel to the shape you want to draw, and perpendicular to
> the direction you want to build in.**

The foot's outline is a plan shape, so it belongs on a face parallel to the ground — the
Top plane, or the sole itself. The fork's ears stand off the side of a limb, so they belong on
that side face. Pick the face the shape naturally lies on and the projection is what you
expect; pick a face at an angle to it and you get a foreshortened shadow that is geometrically
correct and not what you meant.

**When someone picks the wrong one**, the symptoms are consistent enough to teach:

| Symptom | What happened |
| ------- | ------------- |
| Projected geometry is shorter than the thing it came from | drawn on a plane at an angle — you have its shadow |
| Two projected points land on top of each other | the plane is edge-on to the line between them |
| The extrude comes out sideways | right shape, wrong plane — extrude follows the normal |
| The sketch is fine but sits in mid-air | a plane was picked where a face was meant |

**The recovery is not to delete the sketch**, and it has two halves — the second is the one
people miss.

1. Open the sketch, clear the **Sketch plane** field with its ✕, and pick the right face. The
   geometry you drew comes with it.
2. **Delete the `Use` projections and re-project them.** They still point at whatever they
   were projected from, so after the plane moves they are the wrong shadows in the right
   sketch. Re-picking the plane does not refresh them.

Constraints attached to a deleted projection go with it, so expect the sketch to turn blue
again and need re-constraining. That is the moment set-piece 2 pays for itself: drag it, see
what moves, put the constraints back.

The symptom that sends people round in circles is *"I fixed the plane and it is still wrong."*
The plane was never the whole problem.

> Reported from experience by the course author rather than tested here — worth reproducing
> once for the screenshots, not for the finding.

### 5 — Construction geometry: when to draw it, and when not to

Construction geometry is drawn but not built: dash-dot on screen, invisible to every feature.
It exists to hold things that guide geometry without being part of it — axes, centerlines,
symmetry lines, a distance worth naming.

**Draw one when nothing else provides what you need.** The clearest case is a **revolve axis**.
A profile can sometimes be revolved about one of its own edges — the half-disc hands do exactly
that — but only when an edge happens to lie on the axis. A profile set away from its axis has
no such edge, and even when there is one, a dedicated axis line survives a change to the
profile that a borrowed edge does not.

**Do not draw one where a real edge already is.** This is where beginners lose time, and the
lesson test measured it: the origin, the two default planes seen edge-on, and a freshly drawn
construction line all sit in the same place and compete for the same click. Onshape will
happily accept a coincident constraint between a line and its own endpoint, glyph and all,
holding nothing. Nothing is wrong with construction geometry there; what is wrong is having two
things in one spot.

So the station heights in this model are edges of real boxes, not construction lines — and the
revolve axes *are* construction lines, because nothing else provides them.

### Fix, and why it is not here

`Fix` grounds an entity so it cannot move. It makes a sketch *look* defined while explaining
nothing about why anything sits where it does, and a student reaching for it is usually
missing a real constraint. Name it, show it, and say when not to use it. The torso is centered
by a **center point rectangle on the origin** — which is what Fix is usually a substitute for.

## The ball and socket joint

Nine of the thirteen joints are the same joint. It is worth building once, properly, and then
copying — so this section is the design, and stage 4 below is only where it lands in the order.

### The move: the socket is the ball, grown by the clearance

Onshape's standalone **Boolean** feature has a **Subtract** operation with an **Offset**, whose
help reads *"Create a gap between the selected faces and remaining parts or surfaces"* — fields
**Offset all**, **Faces to offset**, **Offset distance**, and a **Keep tools** checkbox that
preserves the tool part after the cut.

That turns the joint into three features and **one** sketch:

| # | Feature | What it does |
| - | ------- | ------------ |
| 1 | Sketch the stalk-and-ball half-profile; **Revolve, New** | the ball becomes its own part |
| 2 | **Boolean → Subtract**: Tools = ball, Target = outboard part, **Offset all** = `0.2`, **Keep tools** ✅ | cuts a cavity that *is* the ball plus the clearance |
| 3 | **Boolean → Union**: ball + inboard part | the ball joins the limb it belongs to |

**Order matters.** Union consumes the ball as a separate part, so the subtract has to come
first, and it only survives because **Keep tools** is ticked.

The payoff is that the cavity is **derived from the ball**, not drawn to match it. They cannot
drift apart, because there is only one sphere in the model. The clearance is a single number in
a single field.

### Why not the two obvious alternatives

**Two concentric arcs in one sketch.** The cavity loop completely contains the ball loop, so
Onshape splits them and the cavity stops existing as one selectable region — it becomes the
ball's region plus a crescent 0.2 mm thick, which is not a click target.

**Two sketches, one variable.** Works, but concentricity drops from *structural* to *named*: it
holds only as long as both sketches keep using the same variable. Subtract-with-Offset gets the
structural guarantee back and deletes a sketch.

### The profile

One closed loop, four entities, drawn to the right of the axis:

```
     ┐  ← top of the axis line
     │╲        arc, radius 3, centered on the joint point
     │ ●─ ─ ─  center — the only dimensioned position
     │╱
     ├╯  ← this join is NOT dimensioned
     │        stalk side, 1.5 from the axis
     └──┘
   axis edge — the revolve axis, a real line, not construction geometry
```

Three dimensions: stalk half-width, arc radius, center height. **The point where the stalk
meets the sphere is found by constraint** — the arc's lower endpoint is coincident with the top
of the stalk line, and the solver puts it wherever the current radius says. Change the ball
diameter and the join slides to where it now belongs. That is the whole argument for constraints
in one feature, and it is why this profile is worth teaching rather than just building.

### Numbers

Three of the four are in the Variable Studio and one stays here —
see [the seven the rule brings up with them](#the-seven-the-rule-brings-up-with-them).

| Variable | mm | From | Meaning |
| -------- | -- | ---- | ------- |
| `#ball` | 12 | `#torsoH / 8` | every ball, all nine joints |
| `#stalk` | 6 | `#ball / 2` | the neck of the ball stud |
| `#fit` | 0.08 | typed; the printer decides this one, like the bar diameter | the gap between the ball and the hollow it turns in |
| `#ballLoss` | 0.10 | typed; what a printed ball loses on its radius | how much narrower than 96 % the mouth is drawn |
| `#grip` | 2.2205 | `sqrt((#ball / 2 + #fit) ^ 2 - (0.48 * #ball - #ballLoss) ^ 2)` | how deep the ball's center sits below the socket's face |

**The mouth is the number that does the work, and grip follows it.** The mouth is drawn at 96 % of
the ball, less twice what a printed ball loses on its radius — **Ø11.32** against a Ø12 ball, so
**0.68 mm of interference** drawn and 0.48 left once the print has taken its 0.10 off each side —
and the ball's depth is whatever puts the cavity sphere through a mouth that size. The socket has
to spread by that interference and it springs back behind the ball. Nothing else needs touching.

**Dimensioned the other way round it breaks when a student edits it.** Hold grip at a number and
let the mouth follow, and loosening `#fit` to 0.8 opens the mouth to 109.8 % of the ball: the
interference goes negative and the ball falls out. Holding the mouth at 96 % survives every value
of `#fit`, and the only thing that moves is how deep the ball sits.
[`experiments/runs/2026-08-25-draft9p1/a2-fit.md`](experiments/runs/2026-08-25-draft9p1/a2-fit.md)
has both readings worked at four fits.

**Grip was typed three times before it was derived.** 2.0 until run 1 built it — at 2.0 the 48 mm
robot's mouth is Ø4.996 against a Ø6 ball, which cannot be pushed in without splitting the collar.
1.35 came back from building it. draft9p0 built 3.6, which is not what doubling 1.35 gives. The
expression above reproduces the joint at any `#ball` and any `#fit` without anyone retyping
anything.

**The check is one measurement.** Measure the circular edge of the mouth: **Ø11.52**. That
single number is only right if the cavity radius *and* its depth are both right.

### Every ball joint sits on a boss

**Decided in discussion, not yet drawn.** A short cylinder at each ball joint, doing two jobs at
once: holding the two parts' broad faces apart, and giving the ball stud somewhere to stand.

**The precondition, which is the whole thing:** a boss only buys tilt **if the face it stands on
steps back**. A pad flush with a broad face changes nothing.

**At the neck it is about tilt.** The head's underside is 36 x 30, so its far corner is 23.4 mm
from the axis, and with a 6 mm gap it hits the torso at asin(6 / 23.4) = **15 deg** — well
before the ball's own limit of about 23 deg. A third of the joint's travel is lost to two flat
plates. Ø12 bosses 4 mm apart move the binding radius to 6 and the limit to **42 deg**, so the
joint becomes limited by the joint. The head's underside must be recessed behind its boss for
any of that to hold. Ø12 also keeps the neck looking exactly as sheet 1 draws it.

**At the ankle it is about the foot.** The shin is Ø12, so its rim swings at radius 6 and tilt
was never the problem. What the boss buys is that the socket mouth lifts clear of the foot
plate, so the plate's thickness, shape and *size* stop being ankle decisions — which is what
lets the feet be as big and goofy as the brief asks for.

**At the hip and shoulder it is the stand-off** that the parts sheet still flags in red.

**Rim chamfers.** Once contact is rim-on-face rather than plate-on-plate, a small area carries
the stop. Chamfer both rims — and the same chamfer at the socket mouth is what would buy tilt
past 23 deg, by letting the stalk lean on a slope instead of a sharp edge.

Open: boss diameter (Ø12 to match the drawn neck, or Ø10), how far each boss protrudes, and
whether the hip boss moves the knee and ankle stations.

### Spreading them across the robot

Nine joints, five ball profiles, and — because the assembly makes the copies — only two
mirrors. The mechanisms Onshape actually offers inside one Part Studio:

| Tool | Copies | Placement | Can target a *different* part? |
| ---- | ------ | --------- | ------------------------------ |
| Linear / Circular / Curve pattern, **Part** type | whole parts | regular, or along a curve | **Yes** — New/Add/Remove/Intersect with its own merge scope |
| The same patterns, **Feature** type | re-runs features | same | re-runs the seed, so the seed's scope applies |
| The same patterns, **Face** type | faces | same | stays on its own part; the fastest of the three |
| **Mirror** — part, feature or face | either | reflection | **Yes** — all four booleans, documented for all three types |
| **Transform** — *Copy in place*, then translate / rotate / by mate connectors | whole parts | **anywhere** | **no boolean at all** |
| **Boolean** — Union / Subtract / Intersect | — | — | this is its entire job: explicit Tools and Targets |

The recipe that follows: **make the shape its own part, copy the part, let Boolean do the
merging.** A shape made with Add is already fused into its host and there is nothing left to
copy — which is the mistake that sends people to the forums.

For this robot that means far less than it first appears, because only the **torso** has a
left and a right inside a single part. Every limb is modeled once and inserted twice, so
nothing about the arms, legs, grippers or feet is mirrored at all — see
[One part per unique object](#one-part-per-unique-object).

- **Mirror the ball tool part** to the opposite shoulder or hip before the Union, so one Union
  fuses both sides into the torso.
- **Circular-pattern the relief slits** around one joint's axis. One drawn, four made.

That is the whole of it. The Mirror lesson is still earned, but by its honest use — mirroring
**features inside one part**, not parts inside a studio.

**Do not pattern joints along a limb**, even though the stations are evenly spaced (20 mm on
the arm, 24 on the leg). The elbow is a hinge, so a linear pattern of the shoulder ball would
have to skip an instance; and a patterned wrist ball would be positionally tied to the
shoulder, so lengthening the upper arm would move it for the wrong reason. **Positions come
from the layout sketch.** That rule does not bend for a tool that happens to fit.

### Traps, from the help and the forum

- **Merge scope is not optional.** If new geometry touches exactly one part, that part is added
  automatically; if it touches several there is ambiguity and you must choose. With Add, Remove
  or Intersect and nothing in scope, the feature errors.
- **A patterned Add that touches nothing fails quietly, per instance.** Onshape staff on the
  forum: it reports "Boolean failed" but does not error the whole feature — the instances just
  land as stray separate parts.
- **The seed part counts as one of the tools**, so a patterned part auto-merges with its
  original wherever they intersect.
- **The neck ball must not merge with the head.** At the moment it is created it sits inside the
  head's solid — the cavity is not cut until the next feature. Modeling it as **New** and
  booleaning afterwards sidesteps this entirely, which is the second reason for the recipe.

### Status

**Built twice, not printed.** Run 1 and run 2 both built this joint in Onshape through the GUI;
their notes are in [`experiments/runs/`](experiments/runs/).

**Subtract with Offset applies uniformly over a sphere** — the question the whole design rested
on. Run 2 measured the mouth at Ø5.802586 against Ø5.803 predicted, so the cavity really is the
ball grown by the fit and there is one sphere in the model.

Still unanswered: whether a **Feature pattern** can be re-targeted at a part its seed never
touched. Every UI name in this section that has not been exercised still comes from Onshape's
help pages, and the failure behaviors from a forum thread with two Onshape-staff replies.

Nothing has been **printed**. Fit and grip are values that survived being modeled; the
snap fit itself wants a test coupon before the class prints fourteen of them.

## Risks

- **Fixing a wrong sketch plane is a two-part repair.** Re-picking the plane keeps the drawn
  geometry but leaves stale `Use` projections behind; those have to be deleted and re-made,
  taking their constraints with them.
- **A fully-constrained layout is unforgiving.** A missing constraint at stage 1 surfaces as a
  broken robot at stage 6. That is the lesson and also the thing most likely to strand
  somebody mid-session.
- **Sketch Text is heavier than it looks.** Text on a face becomes many small edges; extruding
  it is slow to regenerate and a common source of failures on a school machine.
- **Loft needs two sketches on two planes before it makes anything.** It is the right extra
  credit and the wrong first feature — an empty preview until both profiles exist.
- **The clevis was redesigned to fit Ø12 rather than the limb being grown to fit it.** See
  *Why the limbs are Ø12* below.
- **A part that turns out to have a handedness costs a whole part.** The foot and gripper are drawn
  symmetric on purpose. If a later flourish — a heel spur, an angled clip — breaks that
  symmetry, the part count goes up and the assembly gains an instance that is not what it
  looks like. Check symmetry before adding detail, not after.
- **Nothing here is printed.** Both joints are modeled and measured; the stub-and-pocket snap,
  the detent wave and the socket's 0.48 mm of interference are numbers that survived being built,
  not numbers that survived being used. They want a test coupon.
- **Six brief errors were found by building, not by reading — in run 2 alone.** Almost all were
  stale numbers carried from a superseded revision: a driver changed and the numbers derived
  from it did not. When a driver moves, recompute everything downstream of it in the same edit.
- **An acceptance check can pass on a wrong part.** A relief slit came out 2.7 mm deep against
  5.5 specified and every headline dimension still passed, because nothing measured the cut's
  z-extent. Ask for the extent of cut faces and the thinnest wall anywhere in the part.
- **The hinge stub is the most fragile feature on the robot.** A Ø3 mm stub cantilevered off
  a 2.5 mm ear, carrying a leg, printed in PLA with layer lines across it. Chamfer the stub's
  leading edge so the blade cams the ears apart rather than the ears being levered open, and
  give the ears enough free length to flex.
- **The detent may be too fine to print, and at 1× it was.** 24 teeth in a band from r4.40 to
  r5.20 is about 1.2 mm of arc per tooth at the outer radius, against a 0.4 mm nozzle. The
  fallback is fewer detents at a wider pitch, which costs only pose resolution, and that is the
  way the joint went, and then came back: 24 wedges on a ring from r6.00 to r10.45 at 2× scale.
  This entry used to end *round bumps beat wedges here, because a wedge's tip is below the
  nozzle's own corner radius and prints as a bump anyway*, and at 12 wedges the crest was wide
  enough to answer that: a flat 0.83 mm across at the ring's inner end and 1.70 mm at its
  outer. At 24 it is not. The two 45° flanks meet before they reach full height anywhere
  inboard of r 7.478 mm, so the ridge is a point over most of its length and holds only a
  0.45 mm flat at its outer end, where the radial draft cuts it off at r 9.70 mm. Whether the
  nozzle lays that down, and what it leaves when it does not, is what the printed part is being
  asked.

See [`modeling-practice`](../.claude/skills/modeling-practice/SKILL.md) for the standard the
reference model is measured against.
