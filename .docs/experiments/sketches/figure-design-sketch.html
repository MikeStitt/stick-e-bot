Design sketch · concept

Stikbot-style figure, 150 mm

A thirteen-joint articulated figure with oversized feet for free-standing balance and C-clip hands that grip a LEGO bar. Every proportion is a fraction of one number — the torso height, 48 mm — chosen because 2⁴ × 3 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16 and 24. No dimension in the figure lands on a fraction of a millimetre, and the stack totals exactly 150.

This is the first sketch, kept as it was drawn. It is the concept the project started from, and the design has moved three times since. The figure is now twice this size — #torsoH 96, a Ø24 limb, a Ø12 ball — and the height is whatever the parts add up to rather than a target of 150.

What survives is the idea, not the arithmetic: one driving number, every other length a fraction of it, and the hand fixed by the LEGO bar because that part is not ours to choose. For the numbers to build to, read the current plan sheets and the build briefs.

Elevations

Detail A — hand, sized to a LEGO bar

Ø3.2 mm is not a design choice. A LEGO bar — the rod a minifigure holds — is 3.2 mm, and every LEGO clip is a C that grips it. Model the bore at Ø3.1 so the plastic pinches, and the mouth at 2.5 mm so it snaps over rather than slides on. Wall 1.6 mm, grip depth 6 mm along the bar.

For a Technic cross-axle instead, the same hand scaled to a Ø4.9 bore, 3.9 mm mouth holds a 4.8 mm axle. Worth modelling as a variable rather than a number, so one figure can be printed either way.

Detail B — the joint, thirteen times over

One ball size everywhere — Ø6 mm (#torsoH / 8), socket mouth 5.0 mm (84%), wall 1.6 mm, with two relief slits so the cup can spread. Thirteen joints: neck, two shoulders, two elbows, two wrists, two hips, two knees, two ankles.

Keeping every joint identical is a modelling decision, not a manufacturing one. Model the socket once and it can be mirrored and patterned onto every limb — which is exactly the lesson those tools exist to teach.

What this teaches

Revolve

Every ball is a half-circle spun about its own flat edge. Thirteen reasons to learn one tool.

Mirror

Model the left side only. The figure is symmetric about one plane, which is the course's own advice for a symmetric part.

Variables

Height drives limb lengths; bar diameter drives the hand. Change one number, get a different robot that still grips LEGO.

Assemblies and mates

Thirteen ball mates, each leaving rotation about three axes — the thing a single-solid model can never demonstrate.
