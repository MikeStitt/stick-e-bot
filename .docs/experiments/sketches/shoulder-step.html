The shoulder, and why it feathers out

Where the Ø12 rod ends and the blade begins, there is a flat face perpendicular to the limb. Its width is not constant — it is 4.5 mm at the middle and exactly nothing at four points.

The same step, two views, two very different numbers

Look at the two elevations. They are the same shoulder on the same part, seen ninety degrees apart:

  • Along the hinge axis — the blade is 11.62 wide against a 12 rod, so the step is 0.19 mm. Practically flush.
  • Across the hinge axis — the blade is 3.0 thick against a 12 rod, so the step is 4.50 mm. A cliff.

Between those two extremes it varies continuously around the perimeter, and at the four marked points it passes through zero — the blade's corners sit exactly on the rod's surface, so the shoulder has no width at all there.

Why it matters, and how much

The pink region in the top drawing is the shoulder face you would actually print. Near the four marks it narrows below one extrusion width, so the slicer cannot lay a bead there. The result is a ragged, stringy edge at those points rather than a crisp corner.

I should be honest about the scale of this: it is a surface-finish problem, not a structural one. The material underneath is the full rod. Nothing is weak — it just will not look clean, and on a part students handle, that edge is where a fingernail catches.

The fix is a chamfer or fillet along the whole transition, which gives the shoulder a defined minimum width all the way round instead of letting it run to zero. It is not fixed by making the blade narrower — that only moves the feather point inward.
