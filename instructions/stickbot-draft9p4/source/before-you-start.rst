Before you start
================

Five minutes of setting up, once, and then you can work straight through.

What you need in front of you
-----------------------------

**An Onshape account.** The free plan does everything in this guide. Sign up at
`onshape.com <https://www.onshape.com/>`_ and pick the free option. It is a full copy of the same
software, on two conditions. Your documents are public: any Onshape user can open one and take a
copy, and nobody but you can change yours. And the free plan is for learning and hobby work, not for
anything you sell.

**A browser, on a computer.** Onshape runs in Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox. It also has a
tablet app, and this guide is not written for it: every page says which key to press, and the
tablet does not have keys.

**A mouse with a wheel**, or a trackpad you are quick on. You will be spinning the model and
zooming in constantly, and a wheel is much less work than a menu.

**About an hour per page.** There are fourteen. You do not have to do them in one sitting, and the
end of every page is a good place to stop.

**A 3D printer, eventually.** Not for any of the modeling — only for the last thing, which is
holding the robot. If you do not have one, a school or a library often does, and the parts print
in any ordinary filament.

Set the units first
-------------------

A new Onshape document arrives in **inches**, and this robot is in **millimeters**. Page 1 sets
that, on its second step, and it does it while the document is still empty — the dialog closes any
sketch you have open, so doing it first saves you re-opening one.

**Display decimals get set to five places** at the same time. That sounds fussy for a robot the
size of a shoebox, and it is the setting that lets you see the difference between 4.996 and 4.9961
later on, when a joint's fit depends on exactly that.

Two words worth having now
--------------------------

**Part Studio.** A tab where parts get built. Everything you draw and extrude happens in one.

**Assembly.** A tab where finished parts get put together and told how they may move.

You need one Assembly and about ten Part Studios, and every one of them lives in a single Onshape
document. The guide never asks you to make a second document.

Where to look when something goes wrong
---------------------------------------

**The feature tree**, down the left. It is the list of everything you have done, in order, and
anything that has gone wrong shows up there as a small badge on the row that broke.

**The bottom-left corner**, which tells you whether the sketch you are in is **fully defined**. A
sketch whose lines have all turned black is nailed down and will not move when a number changes
later. That is the state every sketch in this guide finishes in.

**Ctrl+Z / ⌘Z** undoes anything, including a feature you have already accepted.
